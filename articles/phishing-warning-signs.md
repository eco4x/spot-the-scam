# How to Spot a Phishing Message: 7 Warning Signs

![Illustration of a suspicious message being examined with a magnifying glass](https://jujpazgaxctqmclnshwc3cjr3i.content.toqi.life/v/1/phishing-awareness.png)

Originally published on [Spot the Scam](https://spotthescamguide.blogspot.com/2026/11/how-to-spot-phishing-message-7-warning.html). For the latest version, visit the original post.

A text says your account will be blocked unless you act immediately. An email promises a job, but asks for your details first. A WhatsApp message from a familiar name asks for a code. Could it be a scam?

Phishing is when someone impersonates a trusted person or organisation to trick you into giving away information, sending money, or opening a harmful link or file. It can arrive by email, SMS, social media, WhatsApp or even a phone call.

Here are seven warning signs and what to do when a message does not feel right.

## 1. The message pressures you to act immediately

Scammers often use fear or excitement to rush you: an account will supposedly be suspended, a parcel will be returned, or a prize will disappear. A genuine issue can still be urgent, so check independently rather than assuming the message is real or fake.

## 2. It asks for a password, PIN or verification code

Be wary of anyone asking you to send a password, mobile money PIN, card details or one-time sign-in code. A caller or message claiming to be support is not proof of identity. Never share a verification code just because someone asks for it.

## 3. The sender looks familiar, but the details are off

Look beyond the display name. An email address may imitate a company, while a phone number or social account may be unfamiliar. Even a message from a known account can be suspicious if that account has been taken over. Contact the person or organisation using a number or app you already trust.

## 4. The link leads somewhere unexpected

Do not tap a suspicious link to test it. On a computer, hovering over a link may show its destination, but that alone cannot guarantee safety. Look for unexpected spelling or domains, and be careful with shortened links that hide the destination. Instead, open the official app or type an address you already know into your browser.

A padlock or HTTPS means a connection is encrypted; it does not prove a website or offer is legitimate.

## 5. You are asked to pay before receiving a prize, job or refund

A message offering a reward or opportunity may ask for a registration, processing or delivery fee first. That is a reason to stop and check. Contact the organisation through its official channels before sending money or personal details.

## 6. An unexpected attachment or download appears

An invoice, document or app may be used to install harmful software or steal information. If you were not expecting a file, confirm it with the sender using a separate trusted contact method before opening it.

## 7. The story or request does not fit

A friend suddenly asks you to send money to a new number, or a provider asks you to verify an account you do not have. Pause and check. Poor spelling can be a clue, but well-written messages can be scams too.

## Example: a suspicious account alert

> Example only: Your account will be closed today. Open this link immediately and send us the verification code to keep it active.

The warning signs are the pressure, the unexpected link and the request for a code. Instead of replying or tapping, open the official app yourself or contact the organisation using contact details you already know are genuine.

## What to do with a suspicious message

1. Stop and avoid the links, attachments and phone numbers in the message.
2. Verify independently through an official app, a website address you know, or a trusted phone number.
3. Report the message as phishing or spam using the service where you received it, then delete it.

## Already clicked or shared something?

If you only opened a link and did not enter information or install anything, stay alert for suspicious account activity. If you downloaded or installed something, update your security software and run a scan.

If you shared a password, change it through the real service and change it anywhere else you reused it. Review active sessions and security settings where available. If you shared bank, card or mobile money details, contact your provider promptly using its official number. If a one-time code was shared, check your account for unauthorised access and contact the service if you cannot regain control.

**Bottom line:** Slow down, verify independently and never give someone your PIN or sign-in code because a message tells you to.
